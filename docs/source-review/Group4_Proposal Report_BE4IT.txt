2.  Scope, Introduction and Background of the Project 
A. Scope of the Project:  
The scope of this research focuses on the analysis and development of an agentic AI 
system for automating the monthly financial reporting workflow of DHI . The research 
will examine the current financial reporting procedure, from the financial submission, 
collection, validation, consolidation, analysis and reporting of monthly financial data. It 
will require a knowledge of the functional, data and workflow re quirements of the 
reporting process and be able to design and develop the proposed system based on these 
requirements. 
 
The key areas to be covered in the research will encompass the development of a web 
based system that can be used to submit, manage and track financial data monthly, have 
an automated system for data ingestion, extraction, validation, and consolidation of the 
data submitted for the financial data and have an automated AI based system that will  
analyze the consolidated financial data to detect anomalies, red flags, trends and 
variances. The research will also explore ways to automatically generate financial 
reporting and management reports and dashboards that present processed financial 
information and AI generated insights to the appropriate end users, such as those in the 
financial reporting and management process. 
 
The research will only focus on the identified financial reporting workflow for the month 
and financial data needed for that financial reporting. It will neither cover creation of a

Project Background  
Now a new generation of AI is emerging from the synthesis (generating content from a given 
prompt) paradigm to the concept of autonomous agents that not only synthesize content but can 
plan, use tools, and execute multiple actions with minimal input from t he user (Wang et al., 
2024). It's an important point to make because it's a significant leap in capabilities between the 
AI agent and the agentic AI.  An AI agent is usually modular, task oriented and can be seen as 
a specific and discrete AI system, while  agentic AI is defined as an AI system that can 
dynamically break up a task, maintain memory over time, and feature coordinated autonomy 
between multiple specialised agents that collaborate towards a shared goal (Sapkota et al., 
2025). One area severely impacted by the transition is financial reporting, as financial 
performance of large diversified organisations, holding companies, conglomerates and 
government investment arms are all subject to the same common and recurring headaches that 
is financial consolidation across numerous subsidiaries into timely, accurate and decision ready 
reports. 
 
This transformation is reflected in the world financial technology literature. Gong (2026) points 
out that after the first generation of rule-based algorithms, we then entered the second predictive 
age using machine learning techniques, and now into the th ird agentic age, creation of self 
contained systems that can engage in the entire financial decision making  process from 
perception to reasoning, strategy generation to execution. Since this third generation is 
beginning to grow, the focus of the research has shifted towards developing more complex 
financial systems that have a financial agent with a special ro le assigned to each agent instead 
of a single agent doing all the jobs (Guo et al., 2024).   This body of work mirrors a global 
appreciation of the fact that financial reporting has moved beyond a compliance process, it is 
an input to strategic decision mak ing and entities which are able to consolidate and interpret 
their financial information more quickly have a distinct governance or oversight edge.

Problem Statement 
A monthly financial reporting process takes place with various companies and involves the 
aggregation of financial data, monitoring, verification, consolidation, analysis and presentation 
of financial data. Work, where manual coordination is paramount, involves many management 
finance staff having to chase submissions, verify if the necessary data was submitted, check 
information for consistency and lack of it from different companies, compare variations in 
financial performance, and prepare reports and han douts for management. This also adds up 
additional time and effort needed to accomplish each reporting cycle. 
 
Several companies have multiple companies involved in the process, which can create even 
more challenges, as the reports are submitted at different times or the information is outlined in 
differing ways or missing. At present, financial reports in DHI comp anies are submitted via 
email, in either PDF or Excel format, and this can lead to manual processes when it comes to 
collecting and handling reports. The data must be processed through the efforts of finance staff 
going through every corresponding email at tachment, finding, downloading, perusing and 
decrypting it. The data must then be processed by manual effort of a finance staff searching, 
downloading, reviewing and decryption of corresponding individual emails. Lack of timeliness 
of submission can then lead to delays in later validation, consolidation, analysis and reporting

DHI therefore has a need for an integrated solution to automate submission reminders, track

Introduction 
Financial reporting is a key part of organisations monitoring and performance evaluation, to 
accountability and managing financial decision making. For organisations with multiple 
subsidiaries, the reporting process can be a multi -step task that calls for collecting financial 
information from various sources, verifying complete and consistent, consolidating, analysing  
and rendering the information into one that can be used by management. As Large Language 
Models (LLMs) are becoming more common in financial applications, they are providing 
opportunities to assist various financial activities, including data analysis, fi nancial research, 
risk identification, and decision support. Additionally, the research has grown, which 
underscores the need for the reliable numerical processing, appropriate coordination, protection 
of privacy, and limits and scrutiny of AI's operations  in the context of real -world financial 
applications (Dong et al., 2025). In recent work on LLM autonomous agents, trajectory 
planning, tool usage and maintaining context as a memory has given way to multi -step 
workflows that include actions (Wang et al., 2024). Multi -agent systems like FinCon and 
FinRobot illustrate the ability of dividing complex tasks among specialized agents, as opposed 
to a single generalist model (Yu et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024). 
 
The proposed project aims at implementing an Agentic AI system to automate Financial 
Reporting and Insights processes for DHI companies. The planned system should help the 
monthly reporting cycle, with features like reminders, data collection, data validat ion, 
consolidation, analysis, generation of monthly reports, and presenting of the data in dashboards. 
The conceptual architecture also suggests a structured store within PostgreSQL as well as a 
store for files and generated documents in object storage such as MinIO or other S3 compatible

Literature Review 
 
AI powered automation has progressed from general -purpose large language models (LLMs) 
to LLM-based agents that are equipped with structured capabilities like planning, memory, and 
interactions with external environments, tools, etc. This evolution has sub sequently led to the 
development of multi-agent systems (MAS) that involve specialized agents working together 
to implement complex multi -step workflows. Agentic AI offers greater adaptability and 
autonomy in dynamic and unpredictable environments compared  to traditional rule-based and 
machine-learning systems, which are typically suitable for well-defined and structured tasks as 
they are consistent, reproducible, and easily audited  (Acharya et al., 2025). ReAct shows that 
reasoning can be mixed into actions, allowing an agent to obtain and then observe the results 
of its actions, and then tweak its responses during that action (Yao et al., 2023). Likewise, 
Toolformer demonstrates how  Language Models can be trained to use external tools like 
calculators, search engines, and Question -Answering systems to enhance task completion 
(Schick et al., 2023). Task decomposition, plan selection, reflection, memory, and external 
modules, are also identified as important mechanisms for multi -step problem solving in 
research on planning oriented agents (Huang et al., 2024). There are also reflection -based 
techniques which try to enhance the performance of an agent by analyzing the information 
generated by its previous action and use a textual message as feedback for the next attempt 
(Shinn et al., 2023).  
 
Multi-agent systems are an extension of agentic AI that involve a group of specialized agents 
working together, communicating, coordinating, and collaborating to achieve a shared goal 
such as a complex task. These architectures can be composed of multiple agents for planning, 
validation, document retrieval, data analysis, reporting, communication, and human escalation 
rather than using a generic single -agent architecture.   CAMEL offers insight into how 
structured communication between role-playing agents can facilitate the completion of the task, 
revealing that the definition of roles can indeed help agents cooperate to complete the task (Li 
et al., 2023). Similarly, AutoGen  offers a structure for multiple LLM agents, software tools, 
and human users to communicate via configurable conversations  (Wu et al., 2024). Guo et al. 
(2024) have conducted a wide -ranging survey of multi -agent systems that can be built using

LLM components and identify agent profiling, memory, planning, communication, and 
environmental interaction as key components of the LLM -based multi-agent systems. Human

resolved, and approval of official reports as reliable AI needs also to be reliable, accountable, 
transparent, explained, and governed (National Institute of Standards and Technology [NIST],

1. Agentic AI in Finance 
Building on these general architectures, the financial sector has started using LLM for data 
analysis, investment research, market interpretation, risk analysis, and decision support, and 
research has shifted from answering questions and generating texts to multi-step financial tasks 
using LLM-based agents. They also document ongoing challenges such as numerical reasoning, 
adapting to changing information, multi -agent coordination, privacy, and compliance. In this 
field literature describing and evaluating concrete systems is focused on trading and market 
behaviour. According to Ding et al. (2024), most LLM -powered trading agents integrate 
numerical market data with the unstructured text found in news and filings.   Liu et al. (2026) 
argue that traditional econometric methods are not adequate to incorporate the interactive and 
strategic properties of financial systems and employ LLM -based MAS to simulate market 
participants. The studies indicate that it is possible to have role differentiated financial agents. 
Their aim is to predict or model market behavior, while an internal reporting system is evaluated 
based on validated data, reconcilability and auditing. The difference will matter when reading 
below in terms of h ow the systems below are evaluated as they rely on trading results for 
success reporting.  
 
 
1.1 Multi - agent decision systems: FinCon, FinAgent, and FinMem 
The leading multi-agent framework in this stream is FinCon. This has a hierarchy of manager 
agent and analyst agent which is similar to that used in investment companies to have specialist 
information processed by the analyst, and the outputs from the anal ysts integrated and 
coordinated by the manager (Yu et al., 2024). The hierarchy is designed to enable agents to 
work together in a cross functional manner without all agents communicating with each other. 
Its most transferable feature for the present project is not its investment logic. It is a mixture of 
role separation and explicit restrictions on autonomous actions. An Insights Agent which raises 
an alarm on unexpected spending or falling margins should not be converted to a management 
verdict without going through defined controls first, and significant outputs should be done so.

There are two related systems each dealing with a different design aspect. FinAgent (Zhang et 
al., 2024) is a multimodal trading agent, which handles numerical, textual, and visual market 
information and integrates market intelligence, memory, reflection, and tool augmentation. 
FinMem (Yu et al., 2025) adds a layered memory, which reflects the relevance and timeliness 
of information, consisting of profile, memory and decision-making parts, and the authors claim 
better trading performance in comparison with algorithmic comparison agents on a large real -
world data set. Their limitations are equally shared. All three are assessed on general trading 
skills, with profit targets, so gains in backtest profit may indicate the evaluation context rather 
than the abili ty of the architecture, their evidence for reporting correctness is therefore 
circumstantial. Furthermore, the architecture of multi-agent Fin Con runs counter to the caution 
by Cemri et al. (2025). None of the works reviewed here confirm that the hierarch y, not other 
architectural characteristics, carries the gains. Fin Con, however, does imply that choice of 
model should not be decided in the absence of an agent workflow. What these systems offer 
the project is therefore a set of transferable principles: bounded autonomy, tool-verified inputs, 
and memory of prior periods.  
 
 
1.2 Research and reporting agents: FinSight and FinRobot 
A more directly relevant group of systems targets research and report production. Fin Sight (Jin 
et al., 2026), a multi -agent architecture for end -to-end research and multimodal reports, has 
Data Collection Agents, a Data Analysis Agent, and a Report Gener ation Agent all sharing a 
programmable environment of data, tools, and agents rather than placing data into the LLM's 
text context.  Two design choices are important. The first is executable data, financial tables 
remain as objects and various tools operate on them, enabling the LLM to choose what analyses 
to run and how to interpret them while the calculations happen by tool. The secon d is a Two-
Stage Writing Framework in which structured Chain -of-Analysis segments are produced first 
and then combined into a report.  These findings support staged separation of the stages of 
computation, analysis, and writing, and should be interpreted with caution. The ablation paper 
shows the benefit of separating processing stages within the report pipeline, but does not 
establish th at multiple autonomous agents are necessary (and therefore cannot address the 
concern raised by Cemri et al., 2025).   FinSight is nevertheless the reviewed system most 
similar to the proposed project. Fin Sight provides strong support to the analysis-and-reporting 
portion of the pipeline, but nothing to the collection, validation, and consolidation stages that 
precede it.  FinRobot (Yang et al., 2024) is an open -source platform that includes a Financial 
AI Agents layer, financial LLM algorithms, DataOps and LLMOps layers, and multiple 
foundation models, where more complex tasks are broken down into logical steps instead of 
answering the question without stopping. The equity research implementation of later robots 
divide collection, concept building and thesis synthesis by specialized agents.

2. Financial Reporting and Data Reliability 
 
Building on these design principles, financial reporting is increasingly supported by artificial 
intelligence, and in particular by LLMs, because financial documents contain large volumes of 
structured and unstructured information that must be extracted, i nterpreted, compared, and 
communicated. Financial reporting, however, is different from general text processing tasks. 
One important aspect is that financial information must be numerically accurate, consistently 
represented across sources, traceable to it s original evidence, and reliable enough to support 
decision making. Recent research on financial question answering, financial document 
processing, and more recently agentic financial workflows demonstrates both the potential of 
LLMs in this domain and th e specific limitations that must be addressed before they can be 
trusted with financial data 
 
Financial data ingestion is the first major technical challenge in an automated reporting system. 
Company information may arrive as spreadsheets, PDF reports, tables, or other documents with 
different layouts, terminology, units, and structures. Extracting  financial information is 
therefore not simply a matter of reading text; the system must correctly identify financial 
concepts and associate them with their corresponding values. Hanne et al. (2024) studied LLMs 
used for table-based extraction and interpre tation of information from annual financial report 
tables, assessing ChatGPT -4 and BARD on financial tables from 46 publicly traded Swiss 
companies. It provides evidence that LLMs can be useful for financial-table extraction, as well 
as the difficulties th at can arise from accessing the quantity of information embedded within 
PDFs. Buholzer et al. (2025) similarly studied LLM -based extraction of financial information 
from financial-report tables, and explored the accuracy and error distribution of a few different 
extraction strategies.These papers reinforce the lesson that, when extracting financial table 
values, they should be evaluated instead of assuming an LLM will always correctly interpret a 
financial report table.Taken together, these pieces of work s uggest that LLMs are well suited 
as financial table extractors in semi-structured documents, but should be used as part of a more 
extensive data pipeline. The bigger sources of information become, the more it is difficult to 
ingest them. The sheer size of the annual reports means that simply feeding them into an LLM 
does not ensure that it will find and rationalise the relevant information.It is not enough to do 
the extraction either, as financial sense may need to make mathematical calculations on several 
values from the tables and other text surrounding the data. 
 
After we've harvested and confirmed financial data, the next hurdle is ensuring that data from 
various companies are compatible and can be joined at the hip in terms of scope and definition. 
The first part of FinReporting transforms the information from multiple sources into a common 
schema that incorporates the core framework of the income statement, balance sheet and cash 
flow statement ( Zhang et al., 2026). This directly applies to the proposed Consolidation Agent, 
If one companies report Sales, one companies report Revenue, one companies report Operating

The extensive review of literature mentioned above revealed that it would be possible to break

3. LLM Tools, Visualization, and Evaluation 
Once financial data are established through tool use and recall mechanisms, those reasonings 
still need to be delivered to decision makers. Multimodal AI capabilities have expanded beyond 
simply processing of text to include the generation of visual images , dashboards, and charts 
(Han et al., 2023).   Another type of multimodal systems, Vision Language Models (VLMs), 
work as the visual critics in chart generation pipelines. VLMs combine visual encoders with an 
LLM backbone to judge the generated visual output such as following input instructions, code 
readability, and aesthetics, keeping readability and correctness for the users  (Han et al., 2023). 
Using a VLM as a visual critic is one example of automated evaluation trend, since the 
evaluation of open ended conversational models, retrieval systems, and visual outputs has to 
shift away from static to reference based metrics (Es et  al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2023). When it 
comes to retrieval pipelines automated evaluation appears in the form Ragas, which evaluates 
three independent and comprehensive metrics without ground truth, human provided labels. 
Faithfulness, which verifies whether generated claims are inferable from the retrieved context, 
Relevance, which evaluates the relevance of the output response to the input prompt, and 
Context Relevance, which evaluates the conciseness and relevance of the retrieved passages 
(Es et al., 2 025). At the same time, the LLM -as-a-Judge way of thinking makes use of large 
language models to act as an automated replacement for human experts in pairwise comparison 
tasks or single answer scalar ratings.  
 
Both scalability and interpretability are important for LLM judges, but they are still known to 
suffer from systematic biases and Unpredictable variance (Yagubyan, 2025; Zheng et al., 2023). 
Typical systematic biases include selection in favor of the respo nse presented first,   favor of 
repetitive text, and possible illusory superiority (Zheng et al., 2023). Repeated trial evaluation 
also shows dramatic pairwise -pointwise gap and intra -judge inconsistency. When the scalar 
pointwise scores of the two models are almost the same the  judge will yet pick a statistically 
significant pairwise winner instead of calling it tie (Yagubyan, 2025). Since   automated LLM 
judges have constraints, human oversight is still needed, at the evaluation level extending the 
governance level. Since automated judges excel at cost effective scaling, human evaluation 
remains an essential baseline to evaluate model alignme nt, helpfulness, and safety (Zheng et 
al., 2023). Today, modern human review is shifting from purely passive human scoring towards 
active human-in-the-loop collaboration (Yao et al., 2022). In an interactive agentic context,

Why LangGraph 
LangGraph is the recommended agent framework because it allows each agent's work 
stream to be constructed as a regulated graph of steps, instead of allowing AI free rein, 
thus this directly supports humans in loop.. For instance, the Reporting Agent's work 
can be prevented from publishing a report until either a critic node or a human approves 
it, with the work being automatically taken to a revising loop when a negative result is 
obtained. LangGraph also offers native functionality for stopping a work stream and 
awaiting human approval, fulfilling the desired mandate where a report is not to be 
circulated without human approval. 
 
Why Qwen 
Qwen was chosen as the base model for the agentic financial reporting system over 
finance targeted options like FinGPT and lightweight options like Falcon because the 
systems central   tasks (ingestion, validation, consolidating, and reasoning over 
messages, explaining anomalies detected in the consolidation, creating the final 
manager quality report) require extensive multi -step reasoning and tooluse ability. 
FinGPT is designed for market sentiment classification and generating trading signals, 
not multi-step document reasoning and report creation, while Falcon can handle basic 
routing and tool calling, it would require heavier task specific fine-tuning to derive the 
document interpretation and reporting quality required. This is supported by direct 
findings from the literature surveyed. FinSight (Jin et al., 2026), a comparable system 
that uses multiple agents to collect, analyze, and report on financial data, directly chose 
to use Qwen models (Qwen3 -Embedding for retrieval, Qwen2.5 -VL for chart 
verification). Wang et al. (2024) asserts that effective agents require strong action 
module capability, run code, query data, and do verifiable actions, which directly apply 
to the responsibilities of the set of Consolidation and Insights Agents proposed here.

Agent Layer 
The Agent Layer is comprised of six specialized AI agents that each oversee an 
individual part of the financial reporting process: 
 
Notification Agent: Notification Agent delivers scheduled reminders to each of the 
subsidiary companies. It tracks submission frequencies and modifies message time and 
message type accordingly. It also knows how and whom to push overdue reminders 
escalations to the concerned higher level actors. 
 
Validation Agent: On receiving a company's monthly financial report, the AI 
validation agent would pull the files from MinIO and  would then use the ingested data 
and ensure the required fields are populated on a validation basis. It would detect

during each sprint. The iterative nature of the development process has enabled the prototype 
to be improved upon following the evaluation stage rather than sole completion of the design 
before its performance has been accessed.  
 
Data and Ethical Considerations 
As the proposed system will process financial data the confidentiality of data and security will 
be taken into serious consideration throughout the project. Financial data will not be included 
unnecessarily in prompts to external services and the system is  designed explicitly to be a 
decision support system and not an autonomous financial decision making  system. Human 
review will be needed for all financial decisions and reports produced by the system. The data 
used to evaluate the agent and system will be used only after getting permission from DHI and 
TTPL.

Review past financial submissions and management reports and find patterns  in reporting 
requirements, format of reports; terminology used. Utilize the results of discussion to establish 
a system requirement, user profiles, reporting needs, and assessment needs. 
  
Activity 2: Literature review and research design 
The project involves a literature review and the development of a research design. Become 
familiar with the literature on financial reporting with AI, financial data processing, data 
reliability, tools using LLM, anomaly detection tools, financial visualiz ation tools, and 
evaluation tools. Write a research gap by doing a research and development plan for a project. 
Establish methodology, and suitable methods for evaluating the developed system. 
 
Activity 3: Applied Research on Agentic AI for Financial Reporting  
Incorporate applied research when developing the system to look at which AI model, 
framework, agent structure and financial processing methods are suitable for use. The activity 
will consist of: 
• Analyze appropriate open weight LLMs and agentic AI systems with regards to specific 
financial reporting tasks. 
• Explore LLM based financial data extraction with validation. 
• Analyse anomaly detection, trend analysis and financial explanations generated by LLM. 
• Understand tasks that can be performed with deterministic functions and those requiring 
LLM reasoning. 
• Implement human oversight, validation and approval mechanisms for AI generated 
content. 
 
Activity 4: System architecture, website and data infrastructure development. 
Define and ideate system and platform design, based on the needs and investigations. Create 
the Web Frontend, backend APIs, the database (PostgreSQL), the object storage system
